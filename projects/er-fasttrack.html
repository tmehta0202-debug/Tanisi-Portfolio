The problem

Better information, without overstepping

Emergency rooms lose time to repeated information gathering, and patients are not good judges of their own urgency. The research I read put it at roughly 65% who overestimate and 8% who underestimate how serious their condition is. That creates a hard line to walk: software can help staff get better information sooner, but it must not make clinical calls it isn't qualified to make.

The model

Patients report, software organizes, staff decide

The whole design follows one chain of responsibility. The patient supplies information, the software structures it and flags what may deserve a faster look, and a qualified clinician makes every real decision. The tool never collapses those roles into one.

Patients report

An adaptive intake asks one question per screen and collects structured, patient-reported information.

→

Software organizes

A non-diagnostic flagging engine surfaces what may need review and explains, in plain language, why.

→

Clinicians decide

A nurse dashboard presents it clearly; every priority and clinical call stays with qualified staff.

Process & build

From triage research to a working app

I grounded the design in how triage actually works before building anything, then used Kiro to move quickly from those decisions to a working, dependency-free app, keeping the design choices my own.

  1. Researched real triage protocols

    Read clinical references including ESI high-risk vital signs and NCBI thresholds, and used them to ground every design decision.

  2. Designed three views

    A patient intake, a clinician dashboard, and a case study, so the tool tells its own story as well as doing the job.

  3. Built a non-diagnostic flagging engine

    It marks information as "review recommended" and always explains the reason, rather than scoring or ranking the patient.

  4. Separated reported from measured data

    The dashboard keeps patient-reported answers visually distinct from measured values, and never invents a measurement it doesn't have.

Accessibility

Built to be usable under stress

An ER tool has to work for stressed patients and busy staff, so accessibility was part of the design from the start, not a pass at the end.

Large touch targets

56px targets so the intake is easy to use quickly and accurately.

High contrast & plain language

Readable text and plain wording instead of medical jargon.

Keyboard & screen reader

Full keyboard navigation and ARIA live regions for assistive tech.

Never color alone

Flags use text and icons, not just color, so meaning is never lost.

Outcome. A working prototype that demonstrates a safe triage-support model: flags come with plain-language reasons, patients can't assign their own priority, and the tool never invents a measurement. It shows the full path from research to design decisions to an accessible, shippable build, and the code is public on GitHub.
